Task 7: Word 4

Spot the Case File — second-guess

second-guess (v)
To doubt or question a decision after it has already been made — replaying it, wondering if a different choice would have been better.

Read the three short case files. Click the one that best shows someone second-guessing themselves, then read the explanation that appears.

Case A

After the surgery went perfectly, Dr. Nowak still lay awake replaying every decision, wondering if she should have operated sooner.

Correct — doubting a decision after the fact, even though it worked out fine, is the essence of "second-guessing."

Case B

The resident made her decision, documented it clearly, and moved straight on to the next patient without a backward glance.

Not second-guessing — she made a clear decision and moved on without revisiting it.

Case C

Before choosing a treatment plan, the team discussed three options openly and picked the strongest one together.

Not second-guessing — this is normal deliberation, made before the decision, not doubt after it.

Task 9: Reading

Notes from Bay 3

Read Alicja's reflection on a night shift. As you read, underline (mentally or on paper) every modal verb used to speculate or deduce something.

At 4 a.m., something about Mrs. Nowicka's chart didn't sit right with me — just a hunch, nothing I could point to on paper. Her blood pressure was borderline; it might have been nothing more than the stress of a long labour, or it could have been the first sign of something worse. The attending glanced at the monitor and said only, "It's inconclusive. Repeat it in twenty minutes."


Twenty minutes felt unbearably long. I kept second-guessing myself — should I have flagged it sooner, or was I overreacting to numbers that were probably perfectly normal? Several explanations seemed equally plausible: white-coat anxiety, dehydration, or the early stages of pre-eclampsia. None of them could be ruled out yet.


When the second reading came back, there was nothing ambiguous about it — the numbers were unequivocal. We moved fast, and by sunrise, both mother and baby were stable. Looking back, I still can't say for certain whether it was training or instinct that made me speak up. It must have been a bit of both. Maybe, in the end, it doesn't matter which.

Task 11: Grammar Theory

Modals of Speculation & Deduction

You already noticed these structures doing real work in the text. Let's name them properly.

1. Speculating About Now: Present Deduction

StructureCertaintyExample
must + infinitive Almost certain (positive) She must be exhausted after a shift like that.
can't + infinitive Almost certain (negative) That can't be right — check the chart again.
might / could / may + infinitive Possible, not certain It could be nothing more than dehydration.

2. Deducing the Past: Past Deduction

StructureCertaintyExample
must have + past participle Almost certain (positive) She must have been worried — she called twice.
can't have + past participle Almost certain (negative) He can't have read the chart properly.
might / could / may have + past participle Possible, not certain It might have been a false alarm.

⚠️ Watch out: "must have" here is a deduction (a guess based on logic), not an obligation. Compare: She must have left already (I'm deducing this) vs. She had to leave early (this was an obligation). Don't confuse "can't have" (impossible, past) with "mustn't have" — that combination doesn't exist in this use.

Task 13: Pronunciation

Reducing the Modal (Avery & Ehrlich)

In fast, natural speech, "have" all but disappears after a modal verb — it reduces to a weak /əv/ sound, almost merging with the modal itself.

She MUST'VE been exhausted. /ˈmʌstəv/ — not "must have," clearly separated

It COULD'VE been anything at that point. /ˈkʊdəv/

He CAN'T HAVE read the chart properly. /kænt əv/ — "can't" stays crisp and stressed, since it carries the negative meaning

🔊 Practice: In pairs, take turns reading the three sentences above aloud, deliberately reducing "have" to /əv/. Your partner should listen for whether the modal itself (must / could / can't) is stressed clearly — that's where the meaning lives, not in "have."

Now try it yourselves: take one sentence from Task 12 and read it twice — once fully pronounced, once naturally reduced. Which sounds more like a real doctor thinking out loud?
