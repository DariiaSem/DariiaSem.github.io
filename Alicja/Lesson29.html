Task 6: Reading

Alicja's Patient History Notes

Read Dr. Alicja's reflection on reviewing a new patient's medical history. Notice the verbs used to describe what the patient reported about the past.

When Maya sat down in my office, she brought a binder three inches thick. She told me that she had been experiencing debilitating pelvic pain since she was sixteen. For a decade, various specialists had dismissed her symptoms as "normal cramps" and had advised her to simply take ibuprofen.


Maya noted that the pain had taken a massive toll on her career. She admitted that she had started to downplay her agony because she was tired of being treated like a hypochondriac. She had learned to remain stoic.


I looked at her previous imaging. I explained to her that standard ultrasounds do not reliably show endometrial tissue. I validated her frustration and promised her that we would investigate further. She cried. Not because of the pain, but because it was the first time a doctor didn't tell her it was in her head.

Task 8: Grammar Theory

Tense Alignment in Medical Histories

When reporting what a patient described about their long-term medical history, we use Tense Alignment (Backshifting). Because the reporting verb is in the past (she said, she reported), the events she describes move one step further into the past.

Patient's Direct Words Doctor's Clinical Notes (Aligned)
Present Perfect → Past Perfect
"I have had this pain for years."		 The patient stated she had had the pain for years.
Past Simple → Past Perfect
"Three doctors dismissed me."		 She noted that three doctors had dismissed her.
Present Cont. → Past Cont.
"The pain is ruining my life."		 She complained the pain was ruining her life.
Universal Truth Exception
"Endometriosis is hard to diagnose."		 I explained that the condition is hard to diagnose. (Does not backshift because it is a permanent medical fact).
⚠️ Watch out: If a patient describes a specific event in the past (e.g., "I fainted when I was 16"), it is often left in the Past Simple rather than shifted to Past Perfect, simply to avoid overly clunky sentences, unless establishing a timeline is clinically crucial.

Task 12: Micro-Case Studies

The "Hysterical" vs. The "Stoic"

The Hysterical Label

A patient comes to the ER crying in severe pain. A senior doctor rolls their eyes and whispers to you, "She's just being hysterical. Her scans are clean. Give her a mild painkiller and send her home."

The Stoic Label

A patient with a known chronic illness sits quietly in the clinic. Because they are not crying or complaining loudly, the doctor assumes the flare-up is minor and delays treatment, saying "You seem to be managing fine."

💬 Discussion: "Pain that is invisible on a scan is not the same as pain that doesn't exist." How do you explain this to a colleague who is dismissive in the scenarios above? What words do you use to stand up for the patient?
