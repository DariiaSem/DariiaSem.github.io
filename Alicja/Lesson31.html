Task 3 · Vocabulary

The Language of Fate

Hover over each card to flip it and reveal the definition. We'll use these to discuss human psychology and luck.

Jinx
To bring bad luck to something, often by speaking about it prematurely. (PL: zapeszyć)
Confirmation Bias
The tendency to interpret new evidence as confirmation of one's existing beliefs or theories.
Self-Fulfilling Prophecy
A prediction that causes itself to come true because you act as if it is already true.
Quirk
A peculiar behavioral habit or a strange occurrence.
Rationalize
To attempt to explain or justify behavior or an attitude with logical reasons.
Placebo
A harmless pill, medicine, or procedure prescribed more for the psychological benefit to the patient.
Knock on wood
Said to prevent bad luck after making a confident statement. (PL: odpukać w niemalowane)
Anomaly
Something that deviates from what is standard, normal, or expected.
Task 4 · Reading

Dr. Miller's Missing Cap

Read this anecdote from an attending physician. Pay close attention to how past events are reported (Tense Alignment).

Dr. Miller was a brilliant surgeon, but he had one strange quirk: he refused to operate without his faded, blue scrub cap. During my residency, he told me that he had never lost a patient while wearing it. I tried to rationalize it, assuming it acted as a psychological placebo to calm his nerves.


One Friday night, we were facing a full moon—a known anomaly for overcrowded ERs. Just before a complicated emergency C-section, Dr. Miller panicked. He exclaimed that someone had stolen his cap from the locker room. I assured him that he was the best surgeon in the hospital and that a piece of cotton didn't dictate fate.


"Don't jinx it," he muttered, quickly knocking on wood. He warned me that acknowledging a good streak usually caused it to end.


The surgery went perfectly. Afterward, I asked him if he realized his fear had just been a self-fulfilling prophecy that thankfully didn't come true. He just smiled, pulled the faded blue cap out of his own scrub pocket where he had absentmindedly stuffed it, and said, "Confirmation bias is a hell of a drug, isn't it?"

Task 5 · Comprehension Check

Fact vs. Fiction

1. The narrator believed the cap possessed genuine magical properties.
False — the narrator tried to rationalize it as a psychological placebo.
2. Dr. Miller believed that mentioning his perfect record would bring bad luck.
True — he told the narrator not to "jinx" it and knocked on wood.
3. The surgery went horribly wrong because he didn't have the cap on his head.
False — the surgery went perfectly despite his panic.
4. At the end, Dr. Miller admits that human brains look for patterns to confirm their own beliefs.
True — he references "confirmation bias" jokingly.
Task 6 & 7 · Grammar

Tense Alignment (Backshifting)

When recounting stories, gossip, or medical histories, we shift tenses backward to reflect that the speech happened in the past.

The Event
(He didn't lose patients)
The Speech
("He told me...")
Now
(You recounting the story)
Direct Speech (The moment)Reported Speech (Backshifted)
"I have never lost a patient." (Pres. Perfect)He told me he had never lost a patient. (Past Perfect)
"Someone stole my cap!" (Past Simple)He exclaimed that someone had stolen his cap. (Past Perfect)
"You are the best surgeon." (Pres. Simple)I assured him that he was the best surgeon. (Past Simple)
"I was stuffing it in my pocket." (Past Cont.)He admitted he had been stuffing it in his pocket. (Past Perf. Cont.)
⚠️ Exception (Universal Truth): If the fact is still biologically/universally true *now*, you don't have to backshift.
Example: "The doctor explained that full moons do not cause higher birth rates."

Shift the Narrative

Fill in the blank with the correct backshifted verb. Pay attention to the original tense in brackets.

  • "I am feeling completely fine." → The patient insisted that she was feeling completely fine.
  • "I took the placebo by mistake." → He confessed that he had taken the placebo by mistake.
  • "I have never seen such an anomaly." → The senior doctor mentioned that she had never seen such an anomaly.
  • "We will cancel the surgery if necessary." → They warned us that they would cancel the surgery if necessary.

Task 13 · Role-Play

The Astrological Dilemma

A patient insists on delaying her scheduled C-section by two hours so her baby is born under a "lucky" star sign (Scorpio instead of Libra). You are the doctor.

Patient: "Doctor, we need to wait until midnight. I don't want a Libra baby; it's bad luck for our family."
↓
Doctor: Validate her anxiety, but push back logically regarding OR scheduling and safety.
↓
Patient: "But it's a self-fulfilling prophecy! If I believe it's bad luck, it will be. Can't we just rationalize a delay?"
↓
Doctor: Refuse politely. Explain medical anomalies and risks of delaying surgery. Try to act as a 'placebo' of calm.
