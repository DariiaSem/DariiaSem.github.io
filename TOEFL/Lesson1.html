Task 4: Reading • Academic Passage

The Teeth That Remember

Read once for the argument (3 minutes), then answer. Hover over dotted words for a Polish hint. Watch for paraphrase: the correct answer almost never copies the passage word for word.

Academic passage • Archaeology & dentistry • ≈ 330 words

1  For most of the twentieth century, archaeologists who excavated ancient burial sites treated the build-up of hardened plaque on teeth as an inconvenience. Known as dental calculus, this mineralized deposit was routinely scraped away before skeletons were cataloged, ostensibly to make the bones easier to measure. This habit has since been recognized as a remarkable act of unintentional destruction.

2  The turning point came in the 2010s, when molecular biologists demonstrated that calculus functions as a kind of microscopic time capsule. As plaque calcifies, it entraps bacterial DNA, proteins, and even fragments of the food a person ate, and the mineral matrix shields these molecules from the decay that destroys most organic material. Analyses of calculus from Neolithic and medieval individuals have therefore revealed what these people consumed, which diseases they carried, and how their oral microbial communities differed from our own.

3  One finding in particular has unsettled long-held assumptions. Oral bacterial communities appear to have become markedly less diverse after the adoption of farming, and again after the Industrial Revolution, when refined sugar and flour became staples. Researchers contend that this loss of diversity may have left modern mouths more vulnerable to the cavity- and gum-disease-causing species that now predominate. Not all scientists accept this interpretation, however; critics point out that the sample of well-preserved specimens is small and geographically uneven, which makes broad generalizations hazardous.

4  Nevertheless, the method has opened doors that once seemed permanently closed. Calculus from a Neanderthal individual in northern Spain, who suffered from a dental abscess, contained traces of poplar, a plant that produces salicylic acid, a natural painkiller. Such discoveries suggest that the mouth records far more than a mere inventory of meals.

Task 7: Grammar Discovery

From B2 to Band 6: Sentence Architecture

Do not read rules yet. First compare two versions of the same idea and work out what the second writer did differently. Rules come after you discover the pattern.

Version A (solid B2)

Many people eat too much sugar. This makes more cavities. Dentists think we should tax sugary drinks, and some people agree with this. But I think it may not work.

Version B (Band 6)

The overconsumption of sugar is widely believed to drive the rise in cavities. Dentists have long argued that sugary drinks should be taxed; yet it remains unclear whether such a measure would, in practice, change behavior. Not only is the evidence mixed, but it is also heavily context-dependent.

The Six Moves

MovePatternBand-6 exampleClassic slip
1. Nominalizationverb / adjective → noun phraseThe decline in diversity coincided with the spread of farming.Long chains of "of" and missing articles.
2. Hedgingappear to / tend to / may well / it seems likely thatCalculus appears to preserve DNA for millennia.Hedging every claim, or using casual "maybe" to open a sentence.
3. Negative inversionNot only / Rarely / Never before / Seldom + auxiliary + subjectNot only did the sealant reduce decay, but it was also cheap.Forgetting the auxiliary: "Not only the sealant reduced…".
4. Participle clausesHaving + past participle / Based on… / Given that…Having analyzed forty samples, the team found a pattern.Dangling subject: "Having analyzed, the results show…".
5. Cleft emphasisWhat… is/was… ; It was… that…What surprised the researchers was the speed of the change.Double subject: "What surprised them it was…".
6. Reporting passiveIt is believed that… / X is thought to have + past participleNeanderthals are thought to have used plants medicinally.Using a simple infinitive for an earlier event: "are thought to use".

Tenses for Reporting Research: A Timeline

PAST
NOW
FUTURE
Past perfectBefore 2010, researchers had already discarded calculus.
Past simpleA 2017 study found poplar traces in Neanderthal calculus.

Task 11: Pronunciation • Speaking • Listen and Repeat

Thought Groups, Focus Words, Fast Speech

Based on the Avery & Ehrlich approach to American English: listeners understand you through rhythm first, individual sounds second. In Listen and Repeat you must copy a whole sentence, so you need to hear it in chunks (thought groups), not in single words.

1. Thought groups

Speakers break long sentences into short meaningful chunks, pausing slightly at the slash (/). Remember each chunk, not each word.

2. Focus word

In each chunk, one important content word gets the strongest stress: longer, louder, higher. Function words (the, to, of, and) shrink and sound reduced.

3. Endings count

Dropped -s, -ed and -th endings change grammar. Finish every word, even in fast speech.

A. Listen and Repeat Ladder (bold = focus word)

  • The dental hygienist / will see you / in about ten minutes.
  • Please make sure / that you've signed / the consent form / before the procedure.
  • The seminar on oral health / has been moved / to the library.
  • If you haven't picked up your results yet, / the front desk / can print them.
  • Students are required / to wear protective equipment / in the laboratory.
  • Although the clinic closes at five, / walk-in patients / are welcome until four.
  • Research suggests / that regular brushing / significantly reduces / the risk of decay.
Routine: listen once, look away, repeat the whole sentence, then compare. If you lose the end, your chunks are too small: group the words into meaning, not into syllables.

Dr. Alvarez, Professor of Public Health

This week we are discussing health policy. Several cities have introduced a tax on sugar-sweetened drinks in order to reduce consumption and, ultimately, rates of tooth decay and obesity. Critics, however, argue that such taxes unfairly burden low-income households. Do you think governments should tax sugary drinks? Explain your position and support it with reasons or examples.

Kai

I support the tax. After Mexico introduced one in 2014, purchases of sugary drinks fell noticeably. If a small price signal can change habits, it is a cheap way to protect public health.

Priya

I disagree. Taxes hit low-income people hardest because they spend a larger share of their money on groceries, and many families will simply switch to cheaper sugary alternatives. Better education in schools would change behavior without punishing anyone.

Priya raises a valid concern about fairness (engages a peer), but I believe a well-designed tax can actually protect lower-income households. Kai's evidence from Mexico shows that behavior can change, yet what neither of them mentions is where the revenue goes (NEW idea, cleft). If the money funds subsidized fresh fruit, free dental screenings or clean drinking water in schools, the policy could narrow, rather than widen, health inequalities. Having worked in a dental clinic, I have seen how frequently sugary drinks contribute to decay in teenagers (specific example, participle clause) whose families cannot afford regular care. Education alone, as Priya suggests, tends to be (hedge) slow and unevenly effective, whereas a price signal works immediately. Not only does a tax discourage consumption, but it also generates the funds needed for prevention (inversion). Admittedly (concession), it should be introduced gradually and evaluated rigorously. Overall, I would support it, provided that the revenue is visibly reinvested in public health (proviso).
