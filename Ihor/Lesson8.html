Task 3 · Vocabulary

The Automation Lexicon

Hover over each card to reveal the definition. Twelve target words for today.

Entry-level
At the lowest level of a career; for people with little or no experience.
Boilerplate
Repetitive, standard code that appears in many places with little change.
Displace
To force someone or something out of its usual position or role.
Augment
To make something greater by adding to it (AI augments, rather than replaces).
Deskilling
Losing skills because tools or processes do the thinking for you.
Upskill
To learn new, more advanced skills so you stay valuable.
Talent pipeline
The steady flow of new people who grow into senior roles.
Hallucination
When an AI confidently produces false or invented output.
Commoditize
To turn something unique into a cheap, interchangeable product.
Redundancy
(UK) Losing your job because the role is no longer needed.
Moat
A lasting advantage that protects a business or career from competition.
Oversight
Careful supervision of work; also (confusingly) a mistake caused by not noticing.

Task 4 · Reading

The Missing Rung

Read the column. Notice the highlighted conditional structures — some mix past and present.

Five years ago, Dana's company hired twelve junior developers every autumn. This year, it hired none. "If we hadn't adopted code assistants so quickly, we would still be training a new cohort," she admits. "But if we were less dependent on them now, we wouldn't have cut the programme so easily."

The logic seemed airtight: a tool that writes boilerplate in seconds makes the first-year engineer redundant. Yet senior engineers noticed a problem within months. "If I hadn't spent two years fixing tedious bugs as a junior, I wouldn't understand our legacy system today," says one veteran. "Had it not been for that grind, I wouldn't be able to spot the subtle errors the AI produces."

Critics call it the missing rung: if companies had kept a few entry-level roles, they would have a healthier talent pipeline now. Instead, were a senior engineer to leave tomorrow, nobody would be ready to replace her. Others disagree. "If I were a recent graduate, I would have started building products on my own long ago," argues a startup founder. "AI hasn't killed the junior role; it has only raised the bar."

The data is still unclear. If hiring had continued at the 2022 level, there would be roughly a third more early-career engineers in the market today. But if the industry weren't so cost-driven, it might have invested in apprenticeship instead of automation. Dana sighs: "If only we had thought about who would review the AI's work in ten years."

💬 Comprehension

  • What changed in Dana's company over five years?
  • Why does the veteran engineer value his junior years?
  • What is the "missing rung" and why is it a problem?
  • What is the founder's counter-argument?
  • What does Dana regret at the end?
Task 5 · Comprehension Check

True or False?

Click the button you think is correct.

1. Dana's company still trains junior developers today.
False — "if we hadn't adopted… we would still be training" means they are NOT training now.
2. The veteran engineer thinks the junior grind helped him develop today's expertise.
True — a past cause (the grind) → a present result (he can spot subtle errors).
3. The company kept some entry-level roles.
False — "if companies had kept a few entry-level roles" is an unreal past.
4. The founder believes AI has only made the junior role harder, not extinct.
True — "it has only raised the bar."
5. Dana thinks the decision was fully planned for the long term.
False — "If only we had thought about who would review the AI's work…"

Which "If" Is It?

  • "If we were less dependent on them now, we wouldn't have cut the programme so easily." → condition: present / result: past

Task 7 · Grammar Theory

Mixed Conditionals: Crossing Time Lines

Standard conditionals keep both clauses in the same time zone. Mixed conditionals cross the line: the "if" part and the result belong to different times.

TypeStructureMeaningTech example
Mixed A: past → presentIf + past perfect, would / could / might + base verbA different past would mean a different present.If I had learned Rust earlier, I would be a staff engineer now.
Mixed B: present → pastIf + past simple, would / could / might + have + past participleA different present state would have changed a past result.If I were more confident, I would have asked for that promotion.
Inverted (formal)Had + subject + p.p., would + base / Were + subject + to + base, would have p.p.Same meaning, more formal; "if" is dropped.Had we kept the juniors, we would have a pipeline today.
Without / But forIf it hadn't been for / Had it not been for / But for / Were it not for + nounRefers to a factor that changed everything.But for AI review, that bug would still be in production.
Wishes & regretswish / if only + past perfect (about the past); wish + past simple (about now)Regret about the past or dissatisfaction with the present.I wish I had learned system design. If only I knew more.
Modal nuancewould = certain; could = ability / possibility; might = weaker possibilityControls how strongly you hypothesize.If I had upskilled, I might be safe from layoffs.
⚠️ Watch out:
1) Don't use would in the if-clause: ✗ "If I would have learned…" → ✓ "If I had learned…"
2) Were is preferred over was in formal English: "If I were more experienced…"
3) Check the time words: yesterday, last year, back then → past; now, still, today, these days → present.
4) Mixed conditionals often need a time word to make the cross-over clear: "…I would still be… now."
5) Use unless only with first-conditional logic — not with unreal pasts.

Task 9 · Grammar Practice

Complete the Hypothesis — Hard Mode

Put the verbs in brackets into the correct form. First decide: what is the time of the "if" part, and what is the time of the result?

  • If the company (not / cut) the junior programme, it (still / have) a healthy pipeline today.
  • If I (be) a better communicator, I (get) the senior role last spring.
  • (Not / it / be) for the automated test suite, the release (be) full of bugs right now.
  • I wish I (spend) more time learning algorithms when I (be) a student; I (not / struggle) so much in interviews now.
  • If AI (not / become) so capable, we (probably / still / hire) interns this summer.
  • (I / know) how fast the market would change, I (not / ignore) cloud skills back then.
  • If she (not / be) so cautious by nature, she (take) the startup offer two years ago.
  • (Were / it) not for mentorship, many seniors (never / reach) their current level — and we (not / have) any leaders today.
1. hadn't cut / would still have
2. were / would have got (AmE: gotten)
3. Had it not been / would be
4. had spent / was / wouldn't be struggling (wouldn't struggle)
5. hadn't become / would probably still be hiring
6. Had I known / wouldn't have ignored
7. weren't / would have taken
8. Were it not / would never have reached / wouldn't have

Task 12 · Pronunciation

Fast Speech: Contracted Conditionals

In natural speech, had, would and have shrink dramatically. Click 🔊, then copy the rhythm.

If I'd learned it earlier, I'd be a lead by now. (/aɪd/ · /aɪd/)
If I were braver, I'd have asked. (/aɪdəv/)
Had it not been for the tests, we'd have shipped bugs. (/wiːdəv/)
She wouldn't have taken the job if she'd known. (/wʊdntəv/)
We might have hired more juniors. (/maɪtəv/)
⚠️ Spelling trap: "would've" sounds like "would of" — but "of" is never correct in writing.

Task 14 · Role-Play

The Hiring Freeze Meeting

An engineering manager and a CTO discuss whether to reopen junior hiring. Both must use mixed conditionals.

CTO: Explain why you froze junior hiring last year: "If we hadn't automated X, we would…"
↓
Manager: Describe present problems caused by that decision: "If we had kept our juniors, we wouldn't be…"
↓
CTO: Defend the decision with budget, risk and AI productivity arguments.
↓
Manager: Propose an apprenticeship model: "Were we to start a programme now, we would…"
↓
CTO: Ask what would happen if it failed. Express a regret: "I wish we had…"
↓
Manager: Summarize a compromise and next steps.
