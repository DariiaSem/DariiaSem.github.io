Task 4 · Reading

The Stoic's Guide to Culture Shock

Read the short story below. Notice the highlighted verb + preposition combinations — we'll come back to them.

Two thousand years ago, the Roman emperor Marcus Aurelius wrote to himself: "You have power over your mind, not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength." He never met a software engineer named Yuriy, but he might as well have.


Yuriy left Lviv for Valencia eight months ago, bringing two suitcases, a laptop, and a very stubborn cat. The first six weeks were what psychologists call the "honeymoon phase" — everything felt like an adventure, even the bureaucracy. Then came the crash. Simple tasks became exhausting. He couldn't find his favourite bread. Nobody understood his jokes. He had to cope with a wave of homesickness he hadn't expected, and some nights he wondered whether he'd made a terrible mistake.


What got him through wasn't optimism — it was Stoicism. He stopped trying to control things he couldn't control: the bureaucracy, the accent, the strangers who spoke too fast. Instead, he focused on what he could control — his habits, his attitude, his Spanish flashcards. Slowly, he began to adjust to a new rhythm: café con leche at nine, stand-up meetings at ten, the beach at sunset on Fridays.


He hasn't fully recovered from missing his family, and he still worries about forgetting his own language. But he has learned to rely on a small circle of friends, both Ukrainian and Spanish, and — more importantly — he has started to believe in his own ability to build a life twice.

💬 Comprehension

  • What does Marcus Aurelius's quote suggest about control?
  • What was the "honeymoon phase" like for Yuriy?
  • What triggered his low point?
  • Name two things Yuriy decided he could control.
  • According to the last paragraph, what does he still struggle with?

Task 7 · Grammar Theory

Dependent Prepositions (Verbs)

Many C1-level verbs are inseparable from a specific preposition. There's no perfect rule — the safest strategy is to learn them as one lexical chunk.

PrepositionCommon VerbsExample
WITHcope with, deal with, struggle with, agree withShe's finally learning to cope with the uncertainty.
TOadjust to, adapt to, react to, look forward toIt took months to adjust to the new time zone.
FROMrecover from, suffer from, differ from, escape fromHe's still recovering from the shock of the move.
ONrely on, depend on, insist on, focus onYou can always rely on your closest friends.
ABOUTworry about, complain about, care about, think aboutTry not to worry about things outside your control.
INbelieve in, succeed in, specialize in, engage inShe truly believes in second chances.
FORapologize for, account for, compensate for, long forHe didn't need to apologize for needing time.
⚠️ Watch out: some verbs change preposition depending on meaning — e.g. "agree with someone" vs. "agree on a decision" vs. "agree to a proposal."
