Task 3 · Vocabulary

The Ethics Toolkit

Hover over each card to reveal the definition. These are your eight target words for today.

Opacity
Lack of transparency; difficult to see through or understand.
Accountability
Being responsible for one's actions and having to justify them.
Bias
A systematic, often unintentional, favoritism in decision-making.
Autonomy
The ability to act independently, making one's own decisions.
Interpretability
The degree to which a human can understand why a decision was made.
Liability
Legal responsibility for something, especially damage or loss.
Oversight
Supervision to ensure correct, ethical behavior.
Scrutiny
Close, careful examination.
⚠️ False friend alert: "oversight" has two very different meanings — careful supervision ("regulatory oversight") vs. an accidental mistake ("it was just an oversight"). Context decides which one you mean!

  • "Ask me 'why', and if I'm high, you'll actually get a clear answer." →
  • "I'm what regulators put a company under when they don't fully trust its algorithm." →
Task 4 · Reading

The Verdict Machine

Read the case file below. Notice the highlighted future forms — we'll come back to them.

By 2031, a fictional lender called Meridian Credit will have processed over forty million loan applications without a single human officer reviewing a case personally. Its algorithm — nicknamed "The Judge" — decides who receives a mortgage and who doesn't, and even its own engineers cannot fully explain why.


This is the essence of the "black box" problem: a system so complex that its opacity becomes a design feature, not a flaw. When a rejected applicant asked for a reason last year, Meridian's support team admitted they simply didn't know. Who, then, holds accountability when an algorithm inherits a hidden bias from decades of imperfect lending data?


Regulators are starting to demand interpretability — a legal right to a clear explanation. By next year, several governments will have passed laws requiring exactly that. Engineers, meanwhile, argue that real transparency would let competitors reverse-engineer the model, and that some autonomy for the algorithm is precisely what makes it accurate.


Within five years, most major banks will be relying on similar systems for nearly every lending decision. Nobody is fully certain who carries the liability if The Judge is proven to discriminate at scale, and until stronger oversight exists, these systems will remain under constant public scrutiny. Ten years from now, banks worldwide will be defending decisions that no human being can fully explain.

💬 Comprehension

  • According to the text, what makes "The Judge" a black box?
  • Why couldn't Meridian's support team explain the rejection?
  • What are regulators starting to demand, and why?
  • What's the engineers' counter-argument against full transparency?
  • According to the last paragraph, what's still unresolved about liability?

Task 7 · Grammar Theory

Future Perfect & Future Continuous

Both forms let us project ourselves into a specific moment in the future — but they answer different questions.

NOW
a specific future point
(e.g. "by 2030")
will be + -ing (in progress AT that point)
will have + V3 (already finished BY that point) ✓
FormStructureUsageExample
Future Perfectwill have + V3An action completed before a specific future point.By 2030, most banks will have automated their entire loan process.
Future Continuouswill be + V-ingAn action in progress at a specific future point (or a predictable routine).This time next year, doctors will be relying on AI for most diagnoses.
⚠️ Watch out: "By the time you read this, I'll have finished" (done first) vs. "By the time you read this, I'll be finishing" (still wrapping up, in progress) — the nuance really matters in status reports and incident updates.

Task 12 · Pronunciation

Fast Speech: "Will Have" → "Will've"

In natural speech, "will have" often contracts and reduces to something close to "will've" /wɪləv/ — the "ha" almost disappears. Click 🔊 to hear each phrase, then copy the rhythm.

By then we will've shipped it.
They will've already decided.
It will've changed everything.
We will be launching next quarter.
You'll be hearing from us soon.

Task 13 · Role-Play

The Compliance Meeting

Act out this branching conversation between a regulator and the engineer who built the system in question.

Regulator: Ask the engineer to explain exactly how the model reached its last controversial decision.
↓
Engineer: Admit honestly what can and can't be explained, using "interpretability."
↓
Regulator: Push back — ask who will be held accountable if it happens again.
↓
Engineer: Propose a compromise using "oversight" or "scrutiny."
↓
Regulator: Set a deadline, using a Future Perfect form ("By [date], you will have...").
↓
Engineer: Agree, but add a realistic caveat using a Future Continuous form ("We will be...").
