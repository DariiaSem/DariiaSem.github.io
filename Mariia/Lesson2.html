Task 2: Master Clinical Vocabulary

Ten Essential Terms for the Ward

Review these core medical terms. Pay close attention to their specific clinical meanings and word stress. These will form the foundation for our conversations today.

ward noun

A separate room or area in a hospital dedicated to a particular type of patient or care.

She was transferred to the maternity ward after the successful delivery.

baseline noun

A minimum or starting point used for comparisons, especially concerning medical test results.

We must establish a clear baseline heart rate before starting any new physical therapy.

sedentary adjective

Involving a lot of sitting and very little exercise or general physical activity.

A highly sedentary lifestyle significantly increases the risk of severe cardiovascular disease.

outpatient noun

A patient who attends a hospital or clinic for treatment without staying there overnight.

The clinic handles fifty outpatient appointments every morning before noon.

prognosis noun

The likely course of a disease or ailment; a medical forecast of the likely outcome of a situation.

With immediate surgical intervention, the prognosis for a complete recovery is excellent.

diagnosis noun

The identification of the nature of an illness or other problem by examination of the physical symptoms.

The blood tests finally confirmed the initial diagnosis of severe anemia.

chronic adjective

Persisting for a long time or constantly recurring; specifically referring to an illness or condition.

The patient requires ongoing management and daily care for chronic back pain.

fatigue noun

Extreme tiredness resulting from mental or physical exertion or underlying illness.

Nurses often experience severe fatigue after working double shifts on busy days.

metabolism noun

The chemical processes that occur within a living organism in order to maintain its life.

Regular daily exercise helps to keep your metabolism functioning efficiently and properly.

endurance noun

The ability to endure an unpleasant or difficult process or situation; overall physical stamina.

Working in the emergency department requires incredible mental and physical endurance.

Task 4: Grammar Breakdown

Present Simple and Present Continuous

Clear medical documentation relies on knowing whether an event is a permanent fact or an ongoing, temporary action.

Present Simple

We use the Present Simple to describe permanent situations, established routines, scientific facts, and strict schedules.

Structure Guide
FormFormulaExample
Affirmative Subject + V1 (s/es) The nurse checks the vitals every hour.
Negative Subject + do not / does not + V1 The patient does not eat solid food yet.
Question Do / Does + Subject + V1? Does the night shift start at eight?
Usage in Clinical Settings
  • Routines and Schedules: The pharmacy opens at exactly nine in the morning.
  • Permanent States: She works in the pediatric ward on the second floor.
  • Scientific Facts: The human heart pumps oxygenated blood to the entire body.

Present Continuous

We use the Present Continuous for actions happening exactly at the time of speaking, temporary situations, or rapidly changing states.

Structure Guide
FormFormulaExample
Affirmative Subject + am/is/are + V-ing The surgeons are operating on him right now.
Negative Subject + am/is/are not + V-ing He is not breathing properly at the moment.
Question Am / Is / Are + Subject + V-ing? Are they updating the patient charts?
Usage in Clinical Settings
  • Happening Right Now: Please be quiet because the patient is sleeping.
  • Temporary Situations: I am studying nursing in London just for this semester.
  • Changing States: His blood pressure is dropping very rapidly.
⚠️ State Verbs Warning Verbs that describe states, internal thoughts, possession, or senses generally cannot be used in the continuous form. Examples include know, believe, understand, seem, need, own, belong, and taste.
Correct format: She needs medical assistance immediately.
Incorrect format: She is needing medical assistance immediately.

Task 9: Grammar Breakdown

Present Perfect and Perfect Continuous

These advanced tenses connect the past directly to the present. The choice depends on whether you want to emphasize the final result of an action or the ongoing duration of a process.

Present Perfect

We use the Present Perfect to highlight the result of a completed action that is relevant now, life experiences, or actions in an unfinished time period.

Structure Guide
FormFormulaExample
Affirmative Subject + have/has + V3 The team has reviewed the medical scan.
Negative Subject + have/has not + V3 She has not taken her medicine today.
Question Have / Has + Subject + V3? Have you checked the baseline rate?
Usage in Clinical Settings
  • Results Relevant Now: The doctor has signed the discharge papers. (The result is that the papers are ready now).
  • Life Experiences: I have worked in a psychiatric ward before, so I know the protocols.
  • Unfinished Time: We have admitted ten new patients today, and the day is not over.

Present Perfect Continuous

We use the Present Perfect Continuous to emphasize the duration of an action that started in the past and continues up to now, or an action that just stopped but has visible physical results in the present.

Structure Guide
FormFormulaExample
Affirmative Subject + have/has been + V-ing He has been resting quietly in his room.
Negative Subject + have/has not been + V-ing I have not been sleeping well recently.
Question Have / Has + Subject + been + V-ing? How long has she been waiting here?
Usage in Clinical Settings
  • Focus on Duration: The patient has been coughing for three hours. (This focuses entirely on how long the action has been happening).
  • Recently Stopped with Visible Results: I have been running through the ward, so I am out of breath right now.
Task 10: Result or Duration?

Grammar Application

  • How long you (experience) this sharp pain in your left shoulder?

Task 13: Role-Play Consultation

The Outpatient Scenario

↓

↓
