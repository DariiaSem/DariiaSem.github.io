Task 2: Master Clinical Vocabulary

Ten Essential Terms for the Ward

Review these core medical terms. Pay attention to their specific clinical meanings and word stress. Then, complete the practice exercises below.

ward noun

A separate room or area in a hospital dedicated to a particular type of patient or care.

She was transferred to the maternity ward after the delivery.

baseline noun

A minimum or starting point used for comparisons, especially in medical test results.

We must establish a clear baseline heart rate before starting the therapy.

sedentary adjective

Involving a lot of sitting and very little exercise or physical activity.

A highly sedentary lifestyle increases the risk of cardiovascular disease.

outpatient noun

A patient who attends a hospital for treatment without staying there overnight.

The clinic handles fifty outpatient appointments every morning.

prognosis noun

The likely course of a disease or ailment; a forecast of the likely outcome of a situation.

With immediate surgical intervention, the prognosis for a full recovery is excellent.

diagnosis noun

The identification of the nature of an illness or other problem by examination of the symptoms.

The blood tests confirmed the initial diagnosis of severe anemia.

chronic adjective

Persisting for a long time or constantly recurring; referring to an illness or condition.

The patient requires ongoing management for chronic back pain.

fatigue noun

Extreme tiredness resulting from mental or physical exertion or illness.

Nurses often experience severe fatigue after working double shifts.

metabolism noun

The chemical processes that occur within a living organism in order to maintain life.

Regular exercise helps to keep your metabolism functioning efficiently.

endurance noun

The ability to endure an unpleasant or difficult process or situation; physical stamina.

Working in the emergency department requires incredible mental and physical endurance.

Task 3: Grammar Breakdown

Present Simple & Present Continuous

Clear medical documentation relies on knowing whether an event is a permanent fact or an ongoing, temporary action.

1. Present Simple

We use the Present Simple to describe permanent situations, established routines, scientific facts, and schedules.

Structure
FormFormulaExample
Affirmative (+) Subject + V1 (s/es) The nurse checks the vitals.
Negative (-) Subject + do/does not + V1 The patient does not eat meat.
Question (?) Do/Does + Subject + V1? Does the shift start at eight?
Usage in Clinical Settings
  • Routines & Schedules: The pharmacy opens at exactly nine in the morning.
  • Permanent States: She works in the pediatric ward.
  • Scientific Facts: The heart pumps oxygenated blood to the body.

Time Markers: always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never, every day, on Mondays.

2. Present Continuous

We use the Present Continuous for actions happening exactly at the time of speaking, temporary situations, or changing states.

Structure
FormFormulaExample
Affirmative (+) Subject + am/is/are + V-ing The surgeons are operating now.
Negative (-) Subject + am/is/are not + V-ing He is not breathing properly.
Question (?) Am/Is/Are + Subject + V-ing? Are they updating the charts?
Usage in Clinical Settings
  • Happening Right Now: Please be quiet; the patient is sleeping.
  • Temporary Situations: I am studying nursing in London this semester.
  • Changing States: His blood pressure is dropping rapidly.

Time Markers: now, right now, at the moment, currently, this week, nowadays.

⚠️ State Verbs Warning Verbs that describe states, thoughts, possession, or senses generally cannot be used in the continuous form. Examples include: know, believe, understand, seem, need, own, belong, taste.
Correct: She needs assistance.
Incorrect: She is needing assistance.
Practice 1: Simple or Continuous?
  • Dr. Evans usually (examine) patients in the morning, but today he (attend) a conference in the city.
  • The baseline heart rate (seem) elevated today. We should monitor it.
  • Currently, the hospital administration (update) the outpatient protocol to improve efficiency.
Task 4: Grammar Breakdown

Present Perfect & Present Perfect Continuous

These advanced tenses connect the past directly to the present. The choice depends on whether you want to emphasize the final result of an action or the duration of an ongoing process.

3. Present Perfect

We use the Present Perfect to highlight the result of a completed action that is relevant now, life experiences, or actions in an unfinished time period.

Structure
FormFormulaExample
Affirmative (+) Subject + have/has + V3 The team has reviewed the scan.
Negative (-) Subject + have/has not + V3 She has not taken her medicine.
Question (?) Have/Has + Subject + V3? Have you checked the baseline?
Usage in Clinical Settings
  • Results Relevant Now: The doctor has signed the discharge papers. (Result: the papers are ready now).
  • Life Experiences: I have worked in a psychiatric ward before.
  • Unfinished Time: We have admitted ten new patients today.

Time Markers: already, yet, just, recently, lately, ever, never, so far, today.

4. Present Perfect Continuous

We use the Present Perfect Continuous to emphasize the duration of an action that started in the past and continues up to now, or an action that just stopped but has visible present results.

Structure
FormFormulaExample
Affirmative (+) Subject + have/has been + V-ing He has been resting quietly.
Negative (-) Subject + have/has not been + V-ing I have not been sleeping well.
Question (?) Have/Has + Subject + been + V-ing? How long has she been waiting?
Usage in Clinical Settings
  • Focus on Duration: The patient has been coughing for three hours. (Focuses on how long the action has been happening).
  • Recently Stopped with Visible Results: I have been running through the ward, so I am out of breath right now.

Time Markers: for (a period of time), since (a specific point in time), how long, all day, all morning.

Practice 2: Result or Duration?
  • The surgeon (operate) for six hours straight; he looks completely exhausted.
  • I (review) all the patient files for tomorrow. They are ready on your desk.
  • How long you (experience) this sharp pain in your shoulder?
  • She (never / administer) this specific medication before, so she asked for guidance.
Task 5: Reading

A Shift in London

Read this diary entry from a nursing student. Pay attention to how she uses different present tenses to describe her routines, experiences, and ongoing actions.

I have been living in London for three months now, and I am finally finding my rhythm on the ward. Every morning, I wake up at six, grab a coffee, and take the tube to the hospital. The routine is intense, but I truly enjoy the challenge. Currently, I am rotating through the outpatient department. It is fascinating because I see a wide variety of cases every single day.


Lately, I have noticed how sedentary many patients are. We are actively trying to promote better fitness habits during their consultations. Right now, I am monitoring a patient who has been struggling with severe fatigue. His baseline tests look normal, so the doctors are investigating other potential causes to form a correct diagnosis. I have already learned so much, but I have been studying late into the night just to keep up with the theory. The prognosis for passing my exams is good, as long as I maintain my endurance!

Analysis
  • Find the grammar: Locate a sentence that describes a temporary situation using the Present Continuous.
  • Find the grammar: Locate a sentence that focuses on the duration of an action using the Present Perfect Continuous.
