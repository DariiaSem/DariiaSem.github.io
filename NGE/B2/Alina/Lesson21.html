Task 7: Word 4

Spot the Case File — insular

insular (adj)
Reluctant to accept outside ideas or contact; narrow-minded and cut off from other perspectives — like an island, detached from the mainland.

Case A

The clinic sends two hygienists a year to observe practices in Germany and openly discusses what to borrow from each visit.

Not insular — this clinic actively seeks outside ideas.

Case B

"We've never needed to look at how other countries do things," the head dentist says. "Our way has worked for thirty years, and it will keep working."

Correct — a flat refusal to consider outside perspectives is the essence of "insular."

Task 8: Word 5

The First-Year Timeline — assimilate

assimilate (v)
To become fully absorbed into a new culture, group, or environment, gradually taking on its habits and rhythms until you no longer feel like an outsider.

A Polish hygienist, Kasia, spent her first year at a clinic in Trondheim. Drag these five moments of her year into the correct chronological order — from first arrival to full assimilation.

  • She begins to imitate small habits — brewing coffee at 10am sharp, joining the Friday cake ritual — without even noticing.
  • Kasia lands in Trondheim, overwhelmed by how quiet the clinic feels compared to home — real culture shock.
  • A new Filipino hygienist joins the team, and Kasia is the one now explaining the clinic's unwritten rules to her.
  • She starts silently observing meetings, noting who speaks first and how disagreement is (or isn't) expressed.
  • Colleagues stop introducing her as "the new Polish hygienist" and simply as "Kasia" — she has fully assimilated.
Task 9: Reading

Notes from Trondheim

Read Kasia's reflection on her first year as a dental hygienist in Norway. As you read, underline (mentally or on paper) any sentence that seems to emphasise one piece of information above the rest.

It seems strange to admit now, but the hardest part of moving to Trondheim wasn't the language — it was learning what people weren't saying. Back home, deference to a senior dentist is deeply ingrained; you simply don't correct someone above you in a meeting. Here, my new colleagues appeared to expect the opposite. What surprised me most was not the silence, but how comfortable everyone was with disagreement. Dr. Aas, the clinic's owner, tends to ask the most junior assistant for an opinion first — precisely because, she once told me, junior staff tend to notice what senior staff have stopped seeing.


It was a patient, Mrs. Holm, who first made me realise how candid Norwegian communication could be. She told me, quite plainly, that she found my explanation of her gum disease "too soft" and wanted the blunt version. I noticed that nobody around me flinched. What I had assumed was rudeness turned out to be simple honesty — no evasions, no cushioning. I began to wonder whether my own instinct to soften bad news was really kindness, or just a habit I had never questioned.


Not every clinic is like this one, of course. A friend who moved to a smaller town told me her practice seemed far more insular — closed off, suspicious of anything "not done here before." What made the difference at my clinic, I think, was curiosity rather than tolerance. It wasn't that my colleagues simply put up with a foreign hygienist; it was that they seemed genuinely to want what I might notice that they couldn't. That is what actually let me assimilate — not silence, not imitation, but being asked, again and again, what I thought.

Task 11: Grammar Theory

Noun Clauses, Reported Thought & Cleft Sentences

You already noticed these structures doing real work in the text. Let's name them properly.

1. Reporting an Impression: seem / appear / tend to

StructureUsageExample
It + seems/appears + (that) + clause Reporting an impression or assumption without stating it as a hard fact. It appears that flat hierarchies are the norm here.
Subject + seem(s)/appear(s) + to + infinitive Attributing an observed quality to one specific person or thing. My colleagues appeared to expect open disagreement.
Subject + tend(s) + to + infinitive Describing a general pattern or habit — not an absolute rule. Junior staff tend to notice what senior staff have stopped seeing.
Noun clause as Subject The whole clause becomes the grammatical subject of the sentence. What patients expect from a dentist varies enormously.
Noun clause as Object The clause functions as the object of a reporting verb (notice, realise, wonder, assume). I noticed that nobody flinched.
⚠️ Watch out: "seem/appear + to + infinitive" describes the subject directly (She seems to know), while "It seems that + clause" reports a more general, detached impression (It seems that she knows). Both soften a claim — neither is used for something you're 100% certain of.

2. Emphasising Information: Cleft Sentences

A cleft sentence splits one idea into two clauses purely to spotlight a single piece of information.

It was
her directness
that shocked me.
What shocked me
was
her directness.
TypeStructureExample
It-cleft It + is/was + [emphasised info] + that/who + rest of sentence It was the bluntness of my colleagues that shocked me most.
What-cleft (pseudo-cleft) What + clause + is/was + [emphasised info] What shocked me most was the bluntness of my colleagues.
All-cleft All + clause + is/was + [emphasised info] All I wanted was a little more deference to seniority.
Negative It-cleft It wasn't... that/who... it was... It wasn't the silence that surprised me — it was the honesty.

Task 13: Pronunciation

Stressing the Spotlight (Avery & Ehrlich)

In a cleft sentence, the whole point is to stress the emphasised information — the "spotlight" word or phrase carries the strongest stress in the sentence, while "it was" / "what was" reduces almost to nothing.

It was HER DIRECTNESS that shocked me. (not her confidence, not her age)

What surprised me most was the SILENCE. (not the language, not the food)

All I wanted was a little PATIENCE. (nothing more, nothing less)

🔊 Practice: In pairs, take turns reading the three sentences above aloud. Your partner should close their eyes and identify which word received the heaviest stress — if they can't tell, exaggerate it further. Note how "was" almost disappears (/wəz/) while the focus word stretches out and rises in pitch.

Now try it yourselves: take one sentence from Task 12 and read it twice, shifting the stressed word each time. Does the meaning change?
