Task 8: Reading

The Art of the Pushback

Read this internal memo from a senior dental assistant regarding scheduling conflicts. Notice how she navigates a highly contentious topic without creating a standoff with management.

To: Dr. Aris & Clinic Management
From: Sarah, Head Assistant
Subject: Observations on Q3 Scheduling

I am writing to discuss our current appointment gaps. While I understand the financial necessity of maximizing patient flow, it could be argued that booking consecutive 15-minute hygiene appointments leaves us vulnerable to delays. When a patient arrives late, the entire afternoon becomes highly contentious.

Yesterday, we reached a standoff with a patient whose insurance was declined. Because the schedule was so tight, the receptionist could not de-escalate the situation properly without delaying Dr. Aris. It is generally assumed that rushing these administrative conversations leads to lower patient retention.

I tend to think we would benefit from a 10-minute buffer every three appointments. Arguably, this would give the staff the time needed to be far more tactful with anxious patients, ultimately improving our clinic's reputation. I would be happy to discuss this further at Friday's meeting.

Discussion:

1. How does Sarah avoid sounding aggressive or bossy in this email?

2. Look at the phrases in italics. If she removed them (e.g., "Booking consecutive appointments leaves us vulnerable"), how would the tone change?

Task 9: Grammar Theory

Hedging & Reported Thought

In professional C1 English, Hedging is the use of cautious language to make claims less absolute. It protects you from being proven wrong and prevents the listener from feeling attacked.

1. Introductory Verbs (Personal Hedging)

StructureEffectExample
I would argue that... Proposes a strong opinion politely. I would argue that this schedule is unsustainable.
I tend to think / feel... Shows a leaning or preference, not a hard fact. I tend to think we need more time per patient.

2. Impersonal Structures (Reported Thought)

By removing "I", you make the opinion sound like a general, objective truth rather than a personal complaint.

StructureEffectExample
It could be said/argued that... Introduces a difficult point neutrally. It could be argued that the reception is understaffed.
It is generally assumed/accepted that... Appeals to common sense or industry standards. It is generally accepted that rushing leads to errors.

3. Softening Adverbs

Arguably
Potentially
Somewhat
Conceivably

"This new material is arguably better, though it is somewhat harder to handle."

🎯 Practice: Turn this blunt statement into a hedged, professional observation: "You are booking too many patients and the staff is angry."
Task 10: Role-Play

The De-escalation Protocol

It's time to put your hedging and vocabulary to the test. Choose one scenario and role-play it with your partner.

Scenario A: The Angry Patient

A patient has been waiting for 45 minutes because the dentist had an emergency. They are threatening to leave and leave a bad review. This is highly contentious.

Your Goal: De-escalate the situation.
Grammar: Use "I tend to agree..." and "It could be said that..."

Scenario B: The Supply Standoff

The clinic manager wants to switch to cheaper, lower-quality gloves. You know these gloves tear easily during procedures. You are in a standoff.

Your Goal: Be tactful but firm in refusing the change.
Grammar: Use "I would argue that..." and "arguably".
