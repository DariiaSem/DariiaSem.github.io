Task 4: Reading • Academic Passage

Why the Fear Stays

Read once for the argument (4 minutes), underline the hedges, then answer twelve questions in the TOEFL style. Hover over dotted words for a Polish hint.

Academic passage • Health psychology • ≈ 370 words (adapted for training)

1  Few medical settings provoke as much apprehension as the dental clinic. Surveys in many countries consistently find that a substantial minority of adults postpone appointments because of fear, and a smaller group avoids treatment altogether until pain forces them to attend. Paradoxically, this avoidance tends to make matters worse: untreated problems grow, and the eventual treatment is more invasive than it would otherwise have been.

2  Psychologists explain the persistence of dental fear largely through conditioning. A single distressing experience, often in childhood, can bind the sights, sounds and smells of the clinic to the sensation of pain, so that these cues alone trigger anxiety years later. Because patients who avoid the clinic never discover that a visit can be painless, the fear is never corrected; the relief of skipping an appointment, in turn, reinforces the avoidance.

3  Perceived control appears to be pivotal. In several experiments, patients who had been taught a stop signal, such as a raised hand, to interrupt a procedure reported less discomfort, even though they rarely used the signal. Researchers interpret this as evidence that anticipated helplessness, rather than the physical stimulus itself, drives much of the distress. Language matters too: describing a sensation as “pressure” rather than “pain” seems to alter how it is experienced, although the effect varies considerably from person to person.

4  Treatment, accordingly, increasingly focuses on gradual exposure rather than persuasion. A patient might first merely visit the waiting room, then sit in the chair, then permit an examination, progressing only when comfortable at each stage. Sceptics note that such programs are time-consuming and that clinics under financial pressure may be reluctant to offer them. Nevertheless, proponents argue that the long-term savings, in avoided emergencies, outweigh the initial investment.

Your discoveries

  1. Which sentences combine a past condition with a present result?
  2. Which sentences have no “if” but still express a condition? What comes first in them?
  3. Find four words or phrases that replace “if”:
  4. Which sentence describes a possible future situation, and which tense does it use after the opener?
  5. In sentence 8, what tense follows wish and it's high time, and does it refer to the past or the present?

1. Sentences 1 and 2 (past perfect in the condition, would + infinitive in the result): mixed conditional.

2. Sentences 2, 3 and 7: they start with Had, Were, Should (auxiliary before the subject = inversion).

3. provided that, otherwise, unless, but for (also possible: as long as, on condition that, supposing).

4. Sentence 7 (and 4, 5): Should + subject + base verb, followed by an imperative or will.

5. The past simple (had, approved), but it refers to the present: an unreal present. This is the “unreal past”.

D. Linking, flapping and weak forms

Linking

A final consonant slides into the next vowel: “check_it_out”, “an_hour_ago”, “turn_off”. Listen for the missing gaps between words.

The American flap

Between vowels, /t/ sounds like a quick /d/: “better”, “water”, “patient”, “data”. Say them with a light tap of the tongue.

Weak forms

“to”, “for”, “of”, “and” shrink to /tə/, /fər/, /əv/, /ən/. Over-pronouncing them breaks your rhythm and your accuracy.

Task 14: Writing • Write for an Academic Discussion

Machine or Mentor?

Ten minutes. Respond to the professor, engage with at least one classmate, and add a new idea. Today, build in at least one structure from Task 6.

Dr. Moreau, Professor of Health Technology

This week we are discussing artificial intelligence in healthcare. Some dental clinics now use software that analyzes X-rays and flags possible cavities. Supporters say it catches early problems that a tired human might miss; critics worry about unnecessary treatment and about professionals losing their own diagnostic skills. Should dental clinics rely on AI to read X-rays? Explain your position and support it with reasons or examples.

Lena

I think clinics should use it. An algorithm doesn't get tired at the end of a long shift, and a second opinion can only reduce the number of missed cavities. If a problem is found early, the treatment is simpler and cheaper.

Omar

I'm not convinced. If the software flags harmless shadows, patients may receive fillings they do not need. And over time, young dentists might never learn to read X-rays properly because they trust the machine.

10:00

Omar is right to worry about over-treatment (engages a peer), but I believe the answer is to use the software as an assistant, not a judge. Lena's point about fatigue is valid; what neither of them has mentioned is patient trust (new idea, cleft). Were a clinic to show the patient the flagged image and explain it (inverted conditional), people would probably understand their treatment better and comply more readily. If I had seen my own X-ray explained that way as a student (mixed conditional), I would not be so anxious now. Admittedly, a flagged shadow is not always a cavity; provided that (alternative to if) a trained dentist confirms every finding, the risk of unnecessary fillings should be small.
