Task 8: Editorial

The Diagnostics of Error

Read the clinical editorial below. Pay close attention to how the author uses Discourse Markers (highlighted in gold) to connect complex ideas, and notice the advanced medical vocabulary.

The Periodontal Review // Complex Cases

No clinician wants to admit fault. Nevertheless, transparency is the cornerstone of modern dentistry. Last week, a patient returned with severe, localized necrosis following a routine extraction. The CBCT scan was highly equivocal—it was unclear whether this was a pre-existing pathology or an iatrogenic complication caused by excessive thermal trauma from the drill. Consequently, the surgical team was forced to halt any definitive restorative plans.

I was reviewing the chart with the Chief Surgeon, and he mentioned that he was going to prescribe aggressive antibiotics. I suggested that doing so without a clear diagnosis might exacerbate the patient's emerging gut dysbiosis. Be that as it may, he felt immediate action was required. Conversely, the patient only requested palliative care, repeatedly stating, "I just want the throbbing to stop."

Navigating these ethical grey areas is the true test of a clinical team. We must balance the urge to intervene with the primary medical directive: Primum non nocere (First, do no harm).

Discussion:

1. Look at the phrase "he mentioned that he was going to prescribe...". Why didn't the author write "is going to"?

2. Which discourse marker above is used to show a result? Which ones are used to show contrast or concession?

Task 9: System Rules

Discourse Markers (C1 Level)

At the C1 level, simple connectors like "but" or "so" are insufficient for academic or clinical writing. We use advanced discourse markers to signal precise logical relationships.

1. Concession (Accepting a point, but introducing a counter-point)

MarkerUsage & MeaningExample
Nevertheless / Nonetheless Despite what has just been said. (Very formal). The scan was equivocal. Nevertheless, we must proceed with palliative care.
Be that as it may Acknowledges a fact, but dismisses it as a reason to change course. ("Even if that is true...") The patient is anxious. Be that as it may, the extraction is urgent.

2. Contrast & Result

MarkerUsage & MeaningExample
Conversely Introduces a statement that is the exact opposite of the previous one. Ice reduces swelling. Conversely, heat will exacerbate it.
Consequently / Thus Shows the direct result or effect. The bur was overheated. Consequently, iatrogenic necrosis occurred.
Task 10: System Rules

Future in the Past & Reported Speech

When reporting conversations from the past about plans that were supposed to happen in the future, we must shift our tenses backwards. This is called the Future in the Past.

"will"
→
would
"am/is/are going to"
→
was/were going to
"am/is/are + V-ing"
→
was/were + V-ing
Direct Speech (At the time)Reported Speech (Now)
The dentist said, "I will extract the tooth." He said that he would extract the tooth.
The manager told me, "We are going to change the protocol." She informed me that they were going to change the protocol.
The patient said, "I am seeing a specialist tomorrow." He mentioned that he was seeing a specialist the following day.
⚠️ Advanced Note: If the plan from the past still hasn't happened yet and is still relevant today, you don't always have to shift the tense backwards. (e.g., "Yesterday he told me he is coming in next week.") However, shifting to "was" is always grammatically safe in formal C1 reporting.

Task 12: Simulation

The Clinical Dilemma

Work with your partner to navigate this clinical scenario. You must address the patient's symptoms while navigating the potential of medical error.

The Senior Assistant

The patient is in agony after a procedure performed by your colleague yesterday. The notes are equivocal. You suspect an iatrogenic injury, but you cannot diagnose it legally. You want to offer palliative advice until the doctor arrives.

Grammar: Use Hedging ("It appears that") and Discourse Markers ("Nevertheless", "Consequently").

The Panicking Patient

You are terrified. The pain is severe, and the hot compress you applied only seemed to exacerbate it. Yesterday, the previous doctor promised you that the pain was going to subside within hours. You demand answers.

Grammar: Report past promises using Future in the Past ("He told me it would...").
