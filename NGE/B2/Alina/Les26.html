The Barber-Surgeon

In the Middle Ages, monks were forbidden from shedding blood. Therefore, the local barber—who already possessed sharp razors—became the town surgeon, performing tooth extractions and amputations.

Discussion: How do you think the psychological experience of going to the "dentist" differed for a patient in 1600 compared to today?

Task 06

The Ether Revolution

Read the historical account of the first successful use of surgical anesthesia in 1846. Pay attention to how the author uses Participle Clauses (highlighted in gold) and Narrative Inversion (highlighted in green) to compress time and add drama.

Historical Archives // October 16, 1846

Prior to 1846, surgery was an inherently macabre spectacle. Surgeons were judged not by their precision, but by their speed, as patients had to be physically restrained to endure the agony. Seldom had the medical community seen anything to challenge this brutal reality, dismissing most pain-relief potions as mere quackery.

However, a Boston dentist named William T.G. Morton was determined to shift this paradigm. Having experimented on himself and his dog, Morton approached the chief surgeon at Massachusetts General Hospital. He proposed using a mysterious vapor—diethyl ether. Desperate for a solution, the surgeon agreed to a public demonstration.

Inhaling the sweet-smelling gas, the patient, a young man with a vascular tumor, soon fell unconscious. The surgeon operated in absolute silence. Not until the patient awoke without memory of the pain did the audience realize that medical history had been rewritten. The era of antiquated, agonizing surgery was over.

Task 08

Narrative Flow: Participle Clauses

At the C1/C2 level, we use participle clauses to combine sentences, express cause/effect, or show chronological order without relying on basic conjunctions like "because," "after," or "and."

1. Present Participle (V-ing)

Used for actions happening at the same time, or to replace "because."

Basic: Because he was desperate, the surgeon agreed.
Advanced: Being desperate, the surgeon agreed.

2. Past Participle (V3)

Used for passive meanings, often replacing "because it was..."

Basic: Because they were judged by their speed, surgeons worked fast.
Advanced: Judged by their speed, surgeons worked fast.

3. Perfect Participle (Having + V3)

Used to emphasize that one action was completely finished before the next one started. It replaces "After he had..." or "Because he had...".

Basic: After he had experimented on his dog, he approached the hospital.
Advanced: Having experimented on his dog, he approached the hospital.

Crucial Rule: The subject of the participle clause MUST be the same as the subject of the main clause. Otherwise, it is a "dangling participle" (a grammatical error).

Task 10

Narrative Inversion

In formal writing and dramatic storytelling, we can place a negative or limiting adverb at the beginning of a sentence. When we do this, we must invert the subject and the auxiliary verb (just like in a question).

The medical community had seldom seen such a brutal procedure.
→
Seldom had the medical community seen such a brutal procedure.
The audience did not realize the truth until he awoke.
→
Not until he awoke did the audience realize the truth.
They had no sooner cut the tissue than the patient screamed.
→
No sooner had they cut the tissue than the patient screamed.

Common Inversion Triggers: Seldom, Rarely, Never, Not only... (but also), No sooner... (than), Hardly... (when), Not until.

Task 11

Inversion Protocol

Rewrite these standard historical statements into highly dramatic, inverted structures.

  • Standard: The barber had rarely performed such a macabre extraction.
    Inverted: (Rarely / he / perform) such a macabre extraction.
  • Standard: He not only invented the drill, but he also discovered anesthesia.
    Inverted: Not only (did / he / invent) the drill, but he also discovered anesthesia.
  • Standard: Humanity did not realize the importance of sterilization until 1865.
    Inverted: Not until 1865 (did / humanity / realize) the importance of sterilization.

Phonetic Autopsy

These five academic/medical terms are notoriously difficult for Ukrainian speakers due to silent letters, shifting stress, and French/Greek origins. Practice them out loud with your instructor.

Macabre
/məˈkɑː.brə/
Trap: The "re" at the end is practically silent (or a very soft 'ruh'). Stress is heavily on the "KAH".
Paradigm
/ˈpær.ə.daɪm/
Trap: The 'g' is 100% silent. Do not say "para-dig-em". It sounds like "para-DIME".
Anesthesia
/ˌæn.əsˈθiː.zi.ə/
Trap: The 'th' is unvoiced (like 'think'). The 's' before 'ia' sounds like a 'z' or 'zh'. Stress is on "THEE".
Quackery
/ˈkwæk.ər.i/
Trap: Ensure you round your lips for the 'qu' (/kw/ sound). Do not say "kvakery".
