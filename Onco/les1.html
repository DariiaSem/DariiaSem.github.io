Task 9 · Grammar Theory

Phrasal Verbs & the Particle Compass

Patients almost never say "exacerbate" or "subside" — they say flare up and die down. Learn these eight two-part verbs first.

Phrasal VerbMeaningExample
come down withto catch / start suffering from (an illness)"She came down with a fever two days ago."
flare upto suddenly become worse or more painful"His pain flares up after heavy meals."
wear offto gradually stop having an effect (e.g. anaesthesia)"The local anaesthetic is wearing off."
pass outto lose consciousness / faint"He passed out in the waiting room."
hold off (on)to delay or postpone something"Let's hold off on surgery until the scan comes back."
clear upto disappear / heal (a symptom or condition)"The rash should clear up within a week."
act upto malfunction / cause trouble (an organ, old injury)"My knee has been acting up in the cold."
creep up onto develop gradually and almost unnoticed"The fatigue crept up on her over months."

The Particle Compass

The little word after the verb (the particle) carries most of the meaning. Group them like this:

⬆️ UP — starting / intensifying

Something begins suddenly or gets worse.

  • flare up
  • act up
  • creep up on

⬇️ OFF / DOWN — fading / delaying

Something weakens, stops, or is postponed.

  • wear off
  • hold off (on)
  • die down

🌊 DOWN WITH — being affected

Catching or being struck by an illness.

  • come down with

💨 OUT / UP — disappearing / consciousness

Losing consciousness, or a symptom vanishing entirely.

  • pass out
  • clear up

Task 11 · Grammar Theory

Word Order: Separable, Inseparable & 3-Word Verbs

Not every phrasal verb behaves the same way. Getting the word order wrong is one of the most common mistakes for advanced learners.

TypeRuleExample
Intransitive (no object)Never split — nothing to place inside."The pain flared up overnight."
Inseparable, 3-wordVerb + particle + preposition always stay together, before the object."She came down with the flu." (never "came the flu down with")
Separable (with object)A noun object can go before or after the particle; a pronoun must go between the verb and particle."Let's hold off the biopsy." / "Let's hold it off."
✅ Correct"The rash will clear it up soon." → wait — with a pronoun object it must split: "The rash will clear up on its own" (intransitive, no object at all).
❌ Incorrect"She came the flu down with." — 3-word verbs never split.
✅ Correct"Let's hold the operation off until Monday." / "Let's hold it off until Monday."
❌ Incorrect"Let's hold off it until Monday." — a pronoun object cannot follow the particle.

Learn Medical Vocabulary in English

Watch this short lesson (engVid, Adam) on core medical terms — wound, cure, heal, disease, and more. Then answer the comprehension questions below by revealing the key idea for each.

🔗 Watch directly on YouTube

1. What is the difference between "sick" and "injured" according to the video?

"Sick" usually refers to illness (a disease, infection, or internal problem); "injured" refers to physical harm from an accident or external cause.

2. What does "heal" describe, and how is it different from "cure"?

"Heal" is the body's natural process of recovering (a wound heals); "cure" means to completely eliminate a disease, often through treatment.

Task 14 · Pronunciation Lab

Stress Patterns & Reduced Forms

Two things native speakers do constantly in casual clinical small talk: they stress the particle in phrasal verbs, and they reduce common filler words. Click 🔊 to hear each item.

1. Particle stress in phrasal verbs

In a two-word phrasal verb, the stress almost always falls on the particle, not the verb.

flare UPnot FLAre up
act UPnot ACT up
wear OFFnot WEAR off
come DOWN withnot COME down with

2. Reduced forms in casual patient talk

Based on Avery & Ehrlich's approach to weak forms: these words almost disappear in fast, natural speech.

kind of / sort of → "kinda" / "sorta" "It's kind of achy" sounds like "It's kinda achy."
going to → gonna /ˈgʌnə/ "We're gonna hold off on surgery for now."
want to → wanna /ˈwɒnə/ "I don't wanna throw up again."
have to → hafta /ˈhæftə/ "You hafta come back if it gets worse."
🎧 Practice: read each example sentence aloud three times, exaggerating the stressed particle, then relaxing it back to natural, reduced speech.
